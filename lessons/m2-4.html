What does the report say, and how do you read it?

Reading a Radiology Report

The parts of a report, how radiologists express certainty, standard categories, and turning a report into plain language

Overview

For most clinicians, the report is the scan. Few will look at the images themselves; they act on what the radiologist wrote. Patients now often read their reports in an online portal before anyone explains them. This lesson walks through Mr. Diaz's CTPA report line by line. About 15-20 minutes.

By the end of this lesson you can:

  • Name the standard parts of a radiology report and what each is for.
  • Explain why you read the impression first, but never only the impression.
  • Interpret common hedging phrases that express how sure the radiologist is.
  • Recognize standard reporting categories such as BI-RADS and Lung-RADS.
  • Explain a simple report to a patient in plain words.

What a report is

A radiology report is the radiologist's written interpretation of the images: what they saw, what they think it means, and what should happen next. It is a permanent part of the medical record and a legal document. It is also, as you saw in Lesson 2.2, the source of the labels for many AI datasets.

Most reports follow the same structure, whether the scan is a chest X-ray or a brain MRI. Many hospitals use structured report templates, with fixed headings and sometimes fixed choices, which make reports easier to read and easier for computers to process.

Click a part of the report

Each dashed box is one standard section. Explore all nine.

Exam

What was done, and when

The exam name, whether contrast was used, and the date and time. Check it matches what you ordered and the right patient. "PE protocol" tells you the contrast was timed for the lung arteries.

Clinical history

The question being asked

Usually copied from the order (Lesson 2.3). It tells you what the radiologist knew when they read the scan. If the history was vague, the read may have been less focused. "Pleuritic" means the pain is worse on breathing in.

Comparison

What it was compared with

Earlier imaging is one of the radiologist's most useful tools: a nodule that has not changed in two years is much less worrying than a new one. "No prior CT" here means nobody knows yet whether the nodule is new.

Technique

How the scan was done

Details of the protocol and contrast. Look for any statement on quality: "adequate opacification" means the arteries filled well with contrast, so a clot could be seen if present. If quality was poor, this is where it is said, and it limits what the report can rule out.

Findings

Everything seen, organ by organ

A systematic description of what the radiologist saw, including normal things and small findings that did not make the impression, like the trace effusion and the opacity at the lung base. A filling defect is a dark gap inside a contrast-filled vessel: the clot. The series and image numbers let anyone find the nodule in PACS. The right-to-left ventricle ratio is checked because a big clot can strain the right side of the heart.

Impression

The bottom line

The radiologist's conclusion, numbered, with the most important finding first. It answers the clinical question ("yes, PE") and lists anything else that needs attention. Many people read only this part. See the next section for why that is risky.

Recommendation

What should happen next

Suggested next steps, often based on published guidelines. The Fleischner Society guidelines say how to follow lung nodules found by chance, based on their size, type and the patient's risk. This line is the one most easily lost after discharge (step 9 in Lesson 2.3).

Communication

Who was told, and when

For critical results, the report documents the direct call: who was told, at what time, and that they repeated it back ("read-back") to confirm. This protects the patient and is a legal record that the finding was passed on.

Signature

Who is responsible

The signing radiologist takes responsibility for the report. Watch for "preliminary" versus "final": overnight preliminary reads can change when finalized, and an addendum is added if something is corrected later.

Impression first, but never only

Reading the impression first is sensible: it gives you the answer in a few lines. But stopping there can miss things. In Mr. Diaz's report, the findings section mentions a small opacity at the lung base and a trace effusion. Neither made the impression, because the radiologist judged them less important, but they help explain his pain when he breathes. A clinician who knows the patient may find a "minor" finding matters a lot.

Speech recognition also makes mistakes. "No evidence of pneumothorax" can become "evidence of pneumothorax," and "left" can become "right." If something in the impression does not fit the findings, or the patient, phone the radiologist. They would much rather you ask.

Think about it

An AI tool that summarizes reports in one line would almost certainly keep the PE and might drop the nodule. What would be lost? Who would notice?

How sure is the radiologist?

Images rarely give a yes-or-no answer. Radiologists express how confident they are with particular phrases, sometimes called hedging language. There is no single agreed scale, and different readers interpret the same phrase differently, which is a real source of confusion. Some departments now publish a lexicon (a fixed list of phrases with approximate meanings).

Hedged phrases are hard for AI too. When a program turns reports into training labels (Lesson 2.2), "cannot be excluded" must become yes, no or "uncertain," and different choices make different models.

Standard categories: the RADS family

For some common problems, radiologists use a scored category so everyone reads the same result the same way. The American College of Radiology publishes a family of these, called Reporting and Data Systems (RADS). Each pairs a number with a risk level and a recommended next step.

SystemUsed forWhat the number tells you
BI-RADSBreast imaging (mammography, ultrasound, MRI)0 (needs more imaging) to 6 (known cancer). 4 and 5 usually mean biopsy.
Lung-RADSLung cancer screening CTHow suspicious nodules are, and when to scan again.
LI-RADSLiver lesions in people at risk of liver cancerHow likely a liver lesion is to be cancer.
TI-RADSThyroid nodules on ultrasoundWhether a nodule needs a needle biopsy or follow-up.
PI-RADSProstate MRIHow likely a significant prostate cancer is.
FleischnerLung nodules found by chance (not part of RADS)Follow-up timing based on size, type and risk. Used for Mr. Diaz's nodule.

You do not need to memorize the categories. Learn to spot them in a report and look up what the number means. They are also a natural target for AI, because the output is a clear category.

Explain it to Mr. Diaz

Mr. Diaz has read his report in the patient portal and is worried. He asks you, the medical student on the team, what it means. Explaining a report in plain words is a skill you will use for your whole career.

Interactive: translate the impression

One way to say it: "The scan found a small blood clot in one of the blood vessels in your right lung. That explains your breathlessness and the pain when you breathe. It is not putting strain on your heart, and the blood thinners we started will treat it. The scan also showed a small spot in your lung, about the size of a pea. Most spots like this are harmless, but we will do another CT scan in 6 to 12 months to make sure it has not changed."

Things to check in yours:

  • Did you answer the main question (clot: yes) first?
  • Did you mention the nodule, and the plan for it, without causing panic?
  • Did you replace words like "segmental," "embolism," "incidental" and "solid nodule"?
  • Did you avoid promising what you do not know (for example, "it's definitely not cancer")?

Reports and AI

Reports connect to AI in three ways you have now seen:

  • As training labels: many datasets mine labels from report text (Lesson 2.2), so unclear or hedged reports make noisy labels.
  • As AI output: some tools now draft parts of the report from the images, or rewrite reports in plain language for patients. The radiologist who signs still owns every word.
  • As a tracking source: software can read the recommendation section to catch follow-ups that would otherwise be lost, like Mr. Diaz's nodule.

Knowledge check

Answer each question to continue. Any answer is fine; read the explanation.

Which part of the report gives the radiologist's overall conclusion?

The impression is the bottom line, ordered by importance. The findings describe everything seen, including smaller things that may not appear in the impression.

A report says "small pneumonia cannot be excluded." What does this most likely mean?

"Cannot be excluded" signals uncertainty. It often leans toward unlikely but leaves the decision to the clinical picture. If it matters, call the radiologist.

You only read the impression of Mr. Diaz's report. Which of these would you miss?

Those findings are only in the findings section. They are minor, but they help explain his pain. Read the whole report.

Key takeaways

  • A report has standard parts: exam, clinical history, comparison, technique, findings, impression, recommendation, communication and signature.
  • Read the impression first, but always read the findings too.
  • Hedging phrases express uncertainty; readers interpret them differently, so ask when it matters.
  • RADS categories and the Fleischner guidelines turn findings into standard risk levels and next steps.
  • Explaining a report in plain words is a core skill. Reports also feed AI as labels, outputs and tracking sources.
