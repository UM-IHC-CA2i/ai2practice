Where is AI already used, and what does it do?

AI in Radiology & Medicine

The AI tools that touch Mr. Diaz's scan, how they share the work with radiologists, AI in the rest of medicine, and generative AI in the clinic

Overview

Module 1 explained how AI works. Module 2 explained how radiology works. This module puts them together and asks what AI changes for patients and for you. We start by looking at the AI tools in use today, first in radiology, then across medicine. About 15-20 minutes.

By the end of this lesson you can:

  • Name the AI tools that can act on one CT scan, from the order to the follow-up.
  • Tell apart four ways AI and a radiologist can share a reading.
  • Give an example of AI in at least three other areas of medicine.
  • Explain what generative AI does well, how it fails, and the rules for using it safely.
  • Find out which AI tools are authorized, using the AI Tool Tracker.

Mr. Diaz's scan, with AI

In Lesson 2.3 you followed Mr. Diaz's CT pulmonary angiogram (CTPA) from the order to the follow-up. Now imagine the same night at a hospital that uses AI. None of these tools replaces a person. Each one does one narrow job at one step.

Most radiology AI does one of four jobs: detect (is something there?), triage (which scans are urgent?), measure (how big, how much?) or draft (put words to it). Each tool on the list was trained and tested separately. A tool that is good at clots knows nothing about nodules.

Who reads first? Four reading designs

At the reading step, the same AI tool can be set up in different ways. What changes is when the radiologist sees the AI output, and who has the last word.

For each card, guess when the radiologist sees the AI's output, then flip it.

ConcurrentAI and radiologist at the same time
ConcurrentAI marks appear while the radiologist reads. Fast, but the marks can pull attention toward what the AI found and away from everything else.
Second readerRadiologist first, AI after
Second readerThe radiologist reads first and reports, then checks the AI output and reconsiders. Slower, but protects independent judgment.
TriageAI decides the order
TriageAI reorders the worklist so likely urgent scans are read sooner. The radiologist still reads every scan. Mr. Diaz's PE tool works this way.
AutonomousAI alone
AutonomousAI makes the call with no human read, for example clearing some clearly normal studies. Rare in radiology so far, and only for narrow tasks.

Think about it

The triage tool misses Mr. Diaz's clot. His scan stays in the normal queue. Does he still get a correct diagnosis? (Yes, but later: a radiologist still reads it. A miss by a triage tool costs time, not the diagnosis.)

AI across medicine

Radiology has the most AI tools of any specialty, but it is not the only one. The ideas from Module 1 apply everywhere: a model learned from examples, it has a sensitivity and specificity, and it can fail on patients unlike the ones it learned from.

Explore: AI beyond radiology
1

Images outside radiology

Any specialty that looks at pictures

  • Eyes: in 2018 the FDA authorized the first autonomous AI system, which checks retina photos for diabetic eye disease in primary care clinics with no eye doctor reading them.
  • Pathology: microscope slides are scanned into digital images, and AI flags areas that look like cancer, for example on prostate biopsies.
  • Skin: tools classify photos of moles and rashes. Many were trained mostly on light skin and work less well on darker skin.
  • Colonoscopy: AI marks possible polyps on the live video during the procedure.
2

Heart signals

ECGs and wearables

  • 12-lead ECG: models can spot a weak heart pump (low ejection fraction) from an ordinary ECG, something cardiologists cannot see by eye.
  • Smartwatches: detect an irregular rhythm that may be atrial fibrillation and tell the wearer to see a doctor. Many alerts are false alarms in young, healthy users (recall PPV from Lesson 1.4).
3

Predictions from the health record

Numbers in, risk scores out

  • Sepsis alerts: scan vital signs and lab results and warn that a patient may be developing sepsis. When one widely used sepsis model was tested at an outside hospital, it missed about two thirds of sepsis cases and raised many false alarms (Wong et al., 2021). Lesson 3.2 returns to this.
  • Deterioration and readmission: scores that predict which ward patients may need intensive care, or who may come back after discharge.
4

Language tools

Generative AI and large language models

  • Ambient scribes: listen to a clinic visit (with the patient's consent) and draft the note. Now common in many health systems.
  • Message drafting: suggest replies to patient portal messages for a clinician to edit.
  • Report drafting: suggest radiology impressions, or rewrite reports in plain language.
  • Chatbots and evidence tools: answer clinical questions. Many students already use them to study.

Generative AI in the clinic

Most of the tools above are narrow: one task, one kind of input, one kind of answer. Generative AI is different. A large language model (LLM) learned from huge amounts of text to predict the next word (Lesson 1.1), and it can write almost anything: a note, a summary, a letter, an answer to a question.

That makes it useful and risky at the same time:

  • Good at: drafting, summarizing, rewording for a patient, and explaining a topic you are learning.
  • Hallucination: it can state things that are false, in fluent and confident sentences, including references to papers that do not exist. It does not know when it is wrong.
  • Omission: a summary can quietly leave out the one detail that mattered.
  • Privacy: anything typed into a public chatbot leaves the hospital. Protected health information (PHI), meaning anything that could identify a patient, must only go into tools your institution has approved.

Four rules for using generative AI:

  1. Never paste anything that could identify a patient into a public chatbot. Use only tools your school or hospital has approved.
  2. Check every fact against the source: the chart, the report, the guideline. Look up every reference.
  3. You own what you submit or sign, whoever drafted it.
  4. Think first, then ask. Lesson 3.3 explains why the order matters.

Which tools are out there?

In the United States, AI that diagnoses or guides treatment is regulated as a medical device by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). The FDA keeps a public list of AI-enabled devices. By mid-2026 it held more than 1,600 devices, and about three in four were for radiology.

The AI2Practice AI Tool Tracker summarizes that list: how many tools there are, which specialties and tasks they cover, and how the numbers have grown each year. Most are narrow tools like the ones in Mr. Diaz's table. Note what the list does not tell you: whether a tool helps patients, or whether it works at your hospital. That is the topic of Lesson 3.2.

Knowledge check

Answer each question to continue. Any answer is fine; read the explanation.

A PE tool moves likely urgent scans to the top of the worklist, and the radiologist still reads every scan. Which reading design is this?

Triage changes the order of reading, not who reads. If it misses a clot, the scan is read later rather than never.

An LLM summary of a report includes a reference with authors and a year. What should you do?

LLMs can invent references that look real. They can still be useful drafts, as long as you check facts and citations against the source.

Which of these is safe to paste into a free public chatbot?

A general question with no patient details is fine (and you should still check the answer). Reports, names, dates and screenshots are protected health information and only go into approved tools.

Key takeaways

  • AI can touch every step of a scan's journey, but each tool does one narrow job: detect, triage, measure or draft.
  • The reading design (concurrent, second reader, triage, autonomous) decides who sees what, when, and who has the last word.
  • AI is also used on eye, skin and pathology images, ECGs, health-record predictions and clinical language.
  • Generative AI writes fluently and can be confidently wrong. Check facts and references, and keep patient information out of unapproved tools.
  • The FDA list and the AI Tool Tracker show which tools are authorized, not whether they help patients.
