3.1
Where is AI already used, and what does it do?

AI in Radiology & Medicine

The AI tools that touch Mr. Diaz's scan, how they share the work with radiologists, AI in the rest of medicine, and generative AI in the clinic

Overview

Module 1 explained how AI works, and Module 2 explained how radiology works. This module puts the two together and asks a harder question: what does AI actually change, for patients and for you? We begin with the tools already in use, first around one radiology patient and then across the rest of medicine.

By the end of the next 15-20 minutes you should be able to name the AI tools that can act on a single CT scan, tell apart the four ways a radiologist and an AI can share a reading, and give examples of AI in at least three other areas of medicine. You will also see what generative AI does well and how it fails, and learn where to look up which tools are authorized.

Part 1 · The scan

Mr. Diaz's scan, with AI

In Lesson 2.3 you followed Mr. Diaz's CT pulmonary angiogram (CTPA) from the order to the follow-up. Picture the same night at a hospital that uses AI. Six different tools could touch his scan along the way, and the first thing to notice is how narrow each one is: none of them replaces a person, and each does one job at one step.

Part 2 · The reading

Who reads first? Four reading designs

At the reading step, the same tool can be set up in quite different ways. The software is identical; what changes is when the radiologist sees its output, and who has the last word. Those two choices decide how much of the radiologist's own judgment gets used on every scan, which will matter a great deal in Lesson 3.3.

Same AI, four setups. The difference is timing and authority.

Before you flip each card, guess when the radiologist sees the AI's marks.

ConcurrentAI and radiologist at the same time
ConcurrentAI marks appear while the radiologist reads. Fast, but the marks can pull attention toward what the AI found and away from everything else.
Second readerRadiologist first, AI after
Second readerThe radiologist reads first and reports, then checks the AI output and reconsiders. Slower, but protects independent judgment.
TriageAI decides the order
TriageAI reorders the worklist so likely urgent scans are read sooner. The radiologist still reads every scan. Mr. Diaz's PE tool works this way.
AutonomousAI alone
AutonomousAI makes the call with no human read, for example clearing some clearly normal studies. Rare in radiology so far, and only for narrow tasks.

Think about it

The triage tool misses Mr. Diaz's clot, so his scan stays in the normal queue. Does he still get a correct diagnosis? (Yes, but later: a radiologist still reads it. A miss by a triage tool costs time, not the diagnosis.)

Part 3 · Beyond radiology

AI across medicine

Radiology has more AI tools than any other specialty, but it is far from the only one. The ideas from Module 1 travel with every one of them: each model learned from examples, each has a sensitivity and a specificity, and each can stumble on patients unlike the ones it learned from. The tabs below show four families.

Explore: AI beyond radiology
1

Images outside radiology

Any specialty that looks at pictures

Any specialty that looks at pictures now has AI tools. In eye care, the FDA authorized the first autonomous AI system in 2018: it checks retina photos for diabetic eye disease in primary care clinics, with no eye doctor reading them. In pathology, microscope slides are scanned into digital images and AI flags areas that look like cancer, for example on prostate biopsies. During colonoscopy, AI marks possible polyps on the live video.

Skin tools classify photos of moles and rashes, but many were trained mostly on light skin and work less well on darker skin, a reminder that a model only knows the patients it learned from.

2

Heart signals

ECGs and wearables

From an ordinary 12-lead ECG, models can spot a weak heart pump (a low ejection fraction), something cardiologists cannot see by eye.

Smartwatches watch all day for an irregular rhythm that may be atrial fibrillation and tell the wearer to see a doctor. In young, healthy wearers, though, many of those alerts are false alarms: the condition is rare in that group, so the positive predictive value is low (recall PPV from Lesson 1.4).

3

Predictions from the health record

Numbers in, risk scores out

These tools read numbers rather than pictures. Sepsis alerts scan vital signs and lab results and warn that a patient may be developing sepsis; other scores predict which ward patients may need intensive care, or who may come back after discharge.

They are easy to over-trust. When one widely used sepsis model was tested at an outside hospital, it missed about two thirds of sepsis cases and raised many false alarms (Wong et al., 2021). Lesson 3.2 returns to that story.

4

Language tools

Generative AI and large language models

Language tools are the newest family, and probably the first you will use. Ambient scribes listen to a clinic visit, with the patient's consent, and draft the note; they are now common in many health systems.

Other tools suggest replies to patient portal messages for a clinician to edit, draft radiology impressions or rewrite reports in plain language, and answer clinical questions. Many students already use chatbots to study.

Part 4 · Generative AI

Generative AI in the clinic

Most of the tools above are narrow: one task, one kind of input, one kind of answer. Generative AI is different. A large language model (LLM) learned from huge amounts of text to predict the next word (Lesson 1.1), and as a result it can write almost anything: a note, a summary, a letter, an answer to a question. That breadth is what makes it both useful and risky.

Where it helps

Drafting, summarizing, rewording a report for a patient, and explaining a topic you are learning. These are real strengths, and they are why the tools spread so quickly.

Where it fails

Hallucination: it can state false things in fluent, confident sentences, including references to papers that do not exist. It does not know when it is wrong.

Omission: a summary can quietly leave out the one detail that mattered.

The danger is not that it sounds wrong. It is that it sounds right.

Privacy

Anything typed into a public chatbot leaves the hospital. Protected health information (PHI), meaning anything that could identify a patient, only goes into tools your institution has approved.

Reasonable

Fine

Not in the radiology report itself, but true to the situation and phrased so the care team decides. A good summary points to the next conversation rather than making promises.

Error

A hallucinated reference

This guideline and paper do not exist. LLMs can produce citations that look real, with authors and years. Look up every reference before you trust or repeat it.

Four rules follow from all of this. They are short enough to carry with you:

  1. Never paste anything that could identify a patient into a public chatbot. Use only tools your school or hospital has approved.
  2. Check every fact against the source: the chart, the report, the guideline. Look up every reference.
  3. You own what you submit or sign, whoever drafted it.
  4. Think first, then ask. Lesson 3.3 explains why the order matters.
Part 5 · Finding the tools

Which tools are out there?

1,600+AI-enabled devices on the FDA's public list by mid-2026, and about three in four of them are for radiology.

In the United States, AI that diagnoses or guides treatment is regulated as a medical device by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), which keeps that public list. The AI2Practice AI Tool Tracker summarizes it: how many tools there are, which specialties and tasks they cover, and how the numbers have grown each year. Most are narrow tools like the ones in Mr. Diaz's table.

Notice what the list does not tell you: whether a tool helps patients, or whether it works at your hospital. Being on the list is not the same as being proven to help. That gap is the subject of Lesson 3.2.

Knowledge check

Answer each question to continue. Any answer is fine; read the explanation.

A PE tool moves likely urgent scans to the top of the worklist, and the radiologist still reads every scan. Which reading design is this?

Triage changes the order of reading, not who reads. If it misses a clot, the scan is read later rather than never.

An LLM summary of a report includes a reference with authors and a year. What should you do?

LLMs can invent references that look real. They can still be useful drafts, as long as you check facts and citations against the source.

Which of these is safe to paste into a free public chatbot?

A general question with no patient details is fine (and you should still check the answer). Reports, names, dates and screenshots are protected health information and only go into approved tools.

The short version

Key takeaways

AI can touch every step of a scan's journey, but each tool does one narrow job: it detects, triages, measures or drafts. How a tool is set up (concurrent, second reader, triage or autonomous) decides who sees what, when, and who has the last word. Beyond radiology, the same ideas apply to eye, skin and pathology images, to ECGs, to predictions from the health record and to clinical language.

Generative AI writes fluently and can be confidently wrong, so check facts and references and keep patient information out of unapproved tools. And the FDA list tells you which tools are authorized, not whether they help patients.

If you remember one line: narrow tools, a human with the last word, and check everything.
